Beyond the
school count.

Victoria’s 2,319 schools serve communities with very different needs. A dot on a map might represent a small primary school or a large combined college. To understand availability, follow the network from where schools are, to whom they serve, to where the young population is projected to grow.

01

A large school network, unevenly distributed.

Schools cluster around Melbourne, and Government schools make up more than two-thirds of the statewide total. Yet each sector’s share of enrolment differs from its share of schools. The network’s size is a useful starting point, not the whole picture.

READ THE DIFFERENCE

Government: 68.7% of schools, 62.2% of FTE. Independent schools show the opposite pattern. Their share of students is larger than their share of schools.

These differences make school type important: a primary school and a combined primary/secondary school are both counted as one school, despite teaching different ranges of year levels.

02

The mix of schools changes by sector and region.

Two-thirds of Independent schools combine primary and secondary education. Government schools, meanwhile, account for a greater share of schools outside metropolitan areas. These differences shape the school types and sectors available in different parts of Victoria.

Fewer schools does not mean fewer places.

Primary and secondary stages cover different numbers of year levels, and schools vary in size. Compare school size next before interpreting the gap as a difference in provision.

03

Fewer schools can still serve many students.

School size helps explain why school counts and enrolment shares differ. Even within the same school type, enrolments vary widely. Full-time-equivalent enrolment (FTE) accounts for part-time study: two half-time enrolments equal one full-time enrolment.

THE MIX CHANGES WITH YEAR LEVEL

The Independent sector’s enrolment is concentrated in combined schools. But provision also differs across school stages: Government’s share is lower in secondary year levels, as the next chart shows.

FROM SCHOOLS TO COMMUNITIES

A school count tells only part of the story.

We have seen why school counts differ from enrolment shares: schools teach different year levels and vary in size.

Now turn from the schools to their communities. Where do young people live, and where could their numbers grow?

04

The network must respond to different communities—and changing demand.

School size explains why counting locations is not enough. The next two maps add community size: one shows young residents per school, the other shows where young people are concentrated. Read together, they distinguish a high ratio from a large population. Then the projections show where that population may grow. Ages 5–19 are a broad comparison group, not an exact student count.

HOW TO READ THIS

Living locally and studying locally are different.

Residents aged 5–19 include non-students; school students can live outside that age band or council area. Whole-school FTE is assigned to the recorded LGA, not split among campuses.

→

Future growth makes the location of provision matter.

Today’s population is only part of the planning challenge. Victoria in Future projects about 104,700 additional residents aged 5–19 between 2026 and 2036. Wyndham, Melton and Casey account for 56% of that statewide net increase, concentrating much of the change in just three communities.

THE PLANNING IMPLICATION

56%

of Victoria’s projected net youth-population increase is in Wyndham, Melton and Casey.

Future demand is concentrated.

These communities need planning for a larger school-age population, including provision across primary and secondary years. Mitchell illustrates another scale of change: a smaller population, but projected growth of 77%.

Population growth measures one driver of future need. It does not tell us how many new schools are required: existing capacity, planned buildings and travel patterns also matter.

GROWING COMMUNITIES

Plan for more young residents.

Wyndham combines the highest current residents-per-school ratio with the largest projected youth increase: about 23,600. This is a reason to examine capacity and planned provision closely, not proof that today’s schools are full.

REGIONAL COMMUNITIES

Preserve access as well as places.

Government schools make up 73.1% of Non Metro school records, compared with 65.8% in Metro areas. Regional provision therefore relies more heavily on this sector. A lower population-to-school ratio does not establish that journeys are short.

CHILDREN MOVING THROUGH SCHOOL

Plan across both stages.

Combined schools and separate primary and secondary schools provide different pathways. Casey has 79 records with primary enrolments and 28 with secondary enrolments, alongside a projected youth increase of about 16,300. Stage counts and school sizes must be considered together.

THE TAKEAWAY

School availability is more
than a school count.

Victoria’s school count is only the beginning. Sector and school type shape the network; enrolment shows its scale; population shows the communities around it. With much of the projected youth increase concentrated in Wyndham, Melton and Casey, planning needs to connect where provision exists with where demand may grow—while retaining access across regional Victoria.
